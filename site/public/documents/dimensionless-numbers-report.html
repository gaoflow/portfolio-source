Dimensionless Numbers technical report

Dimensionless Numbers: a guarded first toolkit

Objective

Written in the first month after moving from software engineering into mechanics (September 2025), this project answers a recurring practical need: every subsequent aerodynamics estimate starts with a Reynolds number, and every hand-checkable Reynolds number should be one command away. The goal was a small, strict toolkit—not a spreadsheet—that:

  1. computes Re, Ma, Pr, Nu (from hh), Gr, and Ra from SI inputs;
  2. refuses dimensionally inconsistent or non-physical inputs with explicit errors instead of silently computing garbage;
  3. carries a tiny fluid-property table (air, water) whose anchors cite a traceable source.

Method

Each group is implemented from its defining equation:

Re=ρuLμ,Ma=ua,Pr=μcpk,Nu=hLk,Re=\frac{\rho u L}{\mu},\qquad Ma=\frac{u}{a},\qquad Pr=\frac{\mu c_p}{k},\qquad Nu=\frac{hL}{k},

Gr=gβ|ΔT|L3ρ2μ2,Ra=gβ|ΔT|L3ρ2cpμk=Gr⋅Pr.Gr=\frac{g\beta\,\lvert\Delta T\rvert L^3\rho^2}{\mu^2},\qquad Ra=\frac{g\beta\,\lvert\Delta T\rvert L^3\rho^2 c_p}{\mu k}=Gr\cdot Pr.

Rayleigh is deliberately written in expanded form rather than as Gr⋅PrGr\cdot Pr, so the identity check is a genuine algebraic test rather than a tautology.

Dimensional guarding uses a minimal SI dimension system. Every parameter declares its dimension vector over the base quantities (M,L,T,Θ)(M, L, T, \Theta); dynamic viscosity, for example, is ML−1T−1M L^{-1} T^{-1}. Plain floats are trusted as SI values but must be finite; Quantity(value, unit) wrappers are checked against the declared dimension and rejected with a message that names the parameter, the expected dimension, and the received unit. Physical-domain guards then enforce positivity (density, viscosity, conductivity, length, speed of sound), non-negativity (velocity), and nonzero temperature difference.

The property table transcribes five anchors from Incropera, DeWitt, Bergman & Lavine, Fundamentals of Heat and Mass Transfer, 7th ed. [1]—Table A.4 (air at 1 atm: 300, 350, 400 K) and Table A.6 (saturated liquid water: 300, 320 K)—with the NIST Chemistry WebBook [2] as the cross-check source. Lookup is exact at anchors, piecewise-linear between them, and refuses to extrapolate. Air’s expansion coefficient is not tabulated per-anchor; it follows the ideal-gas relation β=1/T\beta=1/T.

Results

At sea level (ρ=1.225\rho=1.225 kg/m³, μ=1.81×10−5\mu=1.81\times10^{-5} Pa·s):

  • front-wing chord (L=0.3L=0.3 m) at 15 m/s: Re=3.05×105Re = 3.05\times10^{5};
  • full car (L=5L=5 m) at 60 m/s: Re=2.03×107Re = 2.03\times10^{7}.

The sweep figure plots Re(u)Re(u) on a log axis for the three characteristic lengths, annotated with the flat-plate transition order of magnitude (5×1055\times10^{5}) and a fully-turbulent order of magnitude (10710^{7}). Both annotations are regime intuition, not predictions: real transition depends on surface roughness, pressure gradient, and free-stream turbulence, none of which this toolkit models.

Limitations

  • Constant-property table: five anchors, linear interpolation, no temperature-pressure coupling beyond the ideal-gas β=1/T\beta=1/T for air.
  • Incompressible-regime thinking: nothing here treats compressibility beyond the Mach definition and the perfect-gas speed of sound; no real-gas effects.
  • The regime-limit annotations in the figure are textbook intuition, not transition predictions for any specific geometry.
  • The property table covers air and water only, over narrow temperature ranges; lookup outside the range errors rather than extrapolates.
