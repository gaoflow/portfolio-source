## What I wanted to learn

I used one F1 2026 half-car model to compare how numerical schemes, front- and rear-wing angles, tyre contact patches, ride height, rake, and yaw affected drag, downforce, and aerodynamic balance.

I retained 37 usable cases. I also withdrew four first-version yaw cases after finding an error in the tyre boundary setup.

This is not a production-grade prediction of full-car aerodynamics. The geometry is a third-party reconstruction based on public imagery, the main baseline contains only 4.35 million cells, most steady cases use first-order schemes and high-$y^+$ wall functions, and I have no wind-tunnel or track-correlation data.

I therefore use the results to discuss mechanisms, directional trends, and failure boundaries. I do not treat the absolute coefficients as real RB22 performance.

## Model and computational workflow

The source geometry consists of 14 STEP parts. The full car is 5.40 m long and 1.88 m wide, with a 3.40 m wheelbase. It is not official Red Bull CAD.

The model uses a 50 m/s freestream, a moving ground plane, tyres rotating at 139.3 rad/s, and the $k$-$\omega$ SST turbulence model. Cooling inlets and exhaust paths remain closed as an explicit pilot-scale simplification.

```text
STEP geometry
→ sewing and triangulation
→ snappyHexMesh
→ moving ground and rotating tyres
→ OpenFOAM 14 steady RANS or URANS
→ mesh, conservation, log, and force-window checks
→ full-car and component results
```

The 37 usable cases cover mesh resolution, numerical schemes, turbulence models, front and rear wings, tyre contact patches, ride height, rake, yaw, and wake post-processing.

## Numerical uncertainty comes first

The three mesh levels produced:

| Mesh | Cells | $C_d$ | $C_l$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Coarse | 592,322 | 0.319 | −0.134 |
| Medium | 2,032,685 | 0.260 | −0.252 |
| Fine | 4,351,624 | 0.241 | −0.250 |

Drag changes monotonically with refinement, but the medium-grid downforce passes beyond the fine-grid result. A fourth mesh level exceeded the 15 GB memory limit. These data do not demonstrate mesh independence.

The ends of the force histories look smooth, with short-window confidence intervals of about 0.3%–0.6%. However, extending the averaging window from the final 50 iterations to the final 400 can still change the mean by 15%–19%.

After block resampling, the estimated downforce uncertainty is about 3%–5%. I only interpret a physical change as a clear trend when it is meaningfully larger than that range.

![Convergence histories for all valid cases](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/convergence_overlay.png)

## First-order numerics changed downforce by about 6%

An early second-order `linearUpwind` case ended in a floating-point exception. I initially concluded that the second-order scheme was unsuitable for the current mesh.

After changing to a stable solver setup, I completed 800 iterations on the same 4.35-million-cell mesh:

| Scheme | $C_d$ | $C_l$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| First-order upwind | 0.241 | −0.250 |
| `linearUpwind` | 0.237 | −0.236 |

The second-order result has 1.8% less drag and 5.6% less downforce. The original failure came from the solver setup, not from the second-order scheme itself.

The first-order cases remain useful for relative comparisons within a consistent configuration, but every physical conclusion must be read alongside this roughly 6% numerical-scheme difference.

## Rear wing: low drag and high downforce are not separate switches

I swept the rear wing through $+8^\circ$, $+4^\circ$, $0^\circ$, $-4^\circ$, $-8^\circ$, and $-12^\circ$.

![Six-point rear-wing polar](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/wing_polar.png)

At $-12^\circ$, rear-wing downforce increased by 38% relative to baseline, while drag increased by 74%. The wing still did not stall.

When I rotated the wing toward lower load, further unloading became small beyond $+4^\circ$. Floor upwash maintained a residual effective incidence, so the drag-reduction potential of a Straight Mode is not unlimited.

Across the complete sweep, full-car drag varied by 11% and downforce by 23%. An active wing therefore has to manage drag, load, and front-to-rear balance together.

## The front wing is a one-way balance lever

| Setting | $C_d$ | $C_l$ | Front-axle share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Front wing unloaded by $+4^\circ$ | 0.2408 | −0.2079 | 8.6% |
| Baseline | 0.2413 | −0.2498 | 24.9% |
| Front-wing incidence increased by $-4^\circ$ | 0.2437 | −0.2328 | 22.2% |

Unloading the front wing by $4^\circ$ reduced the front-axle share by 16.3 percentage points.

Increasing incidence in the opposite direction reduced the leading-edge ground clearance from 53 mm to 34 mm. The front wing’s own load then fell by about 10%, while its drag rose by about 30%. In that direction, the current geometry had already passed beyond its high-load ground-effect region.

Using the unloading sensitivity, I estimated that about $+2^\circ$ of front-wing rotation would compensate for the rearward balance shift caused by the $+8^\circ$ rear-wing setting. The combined case produced a 23.9% front-axle share, only one percentage point from baseline, but reduced total downforce by 22.7%.

![Front- and rear-wing settings against full-car balance](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/balance_map.png)

## The tyre contact patch produced the largest single improvement

In the original geometry, each tyre met the ground along a knife edge. After adding a 15 mm contact-patch plinth, the front-wheel stagnation region and lateral squeeze-flow region became smaller.

Full-car drag fell by 6.1%, downforce increased by 4.4%, and $-L/D$ improved from 1.035 to 1.152—an 11% increase.

![Original contact-region pressure](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/baseline_z035_cp.png)

![Contact-region pressure with a 15 mm plinth](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/plinth15_z035_cp.png)

The 5 mm and 10 mm plinths already reduced drag, but their downforce changes remained inside the 3%–5% uncertainty range. Only the 15 mm case produced clear changes in both drag and load.

I also compressed the tyres downward by 2, 4, 6, and 8 mm. The response was not monotonic. The 6 mm case lost 6.1% of its downforce, while the 8 mm case recovered to a 2.9% loss. The results do not support a simple rule that greater compression always means less load.

## Ride height reached a cliff between 10 and 15 mm

| Body lift | $C_d$ | $C_l$ | Floor $C_l$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 mm | 0.2413 | −0.2498 | −0.0644 |
| +10 mm | 0.2309 | −0.2222 | −0.0571 |
| +15 mm | 0.2079 | −0.1636 | −0.0208 |
| +20 mm | Steady solver diverged twice | — | — |

The first 10 mm of body lift reduced total downforce by 11%. The next 5 mm caused a further 26% loss, while floor load fell by 64%.

![Floor-load loss across the ride-height sweep](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/ride_map.png)

I initially interpreted the two steady-solver failures at +20 mm as evidence of stronger physical unsteadiness.

The later URANS cases rejected that explanation. Both +15 mm and +20 mm advanced stably, and their force oscillations were smaller than those of the baseline.

The original divergence therefore marked the operating boundary of the SIMPLE solver setup. It was not evidence of porpoising or real aerodynamic unsteadiness.

## Why I withdrew the entire first yaw sweep

The first yaw setup combined the left and right tyres into one rotating-wall boundary and assigned them a shared centre of rotation. At $+3^\circ$ and $-3^\circ$, the downforce difference reached 13.5%, violating the sign-reversal behaviour expected from a symmetric model.

I withdrew all four first-version yaw cases. In the corrected setup, each of the four tyres has its own patch and centre of rotation:

| Yaw angle | $C_d$ | $C_l$ | Front-axle share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| $-3^\circ$ | 0.2768 | −0.1985 | 37.2% |
| $0^\circ$ | 0.2769 | −0.2089 | 25.7% |
| $+3^\circ$ | 0.2754 | −0.1867 | 38.0% |

For the final result, I use the mean of the positive and negative directions. At $|3^\circ|$, drag changes by about −0.3%, downforce falls by 7.8%, and the front-axle share rises by 11.9 percentage points.

![Corrected full-car yaw results](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/yawv2_map.png)

## Both rake directions lost downforce

I pitched the body by $\pm0.3^\circ$ around the front contact point while keeping the tyres on the ground.

- Nose-down at $-0.3^\circ$: downforce fell by 11.4%.
- Nose-up at $+0.3^\circ$: downforce fell by 19.7%, while the front-axle share increased by 9.2 percentage points.

![Rake sweep](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/rake_map.png)

This model does not support the simple claim that more rake always produces more floor load. Front-wing ground clearance, tyre squeeze flow, and the floor inlet combine to create a narrow, asymmetric attitude window.

## Full-car totals can hide large local changes

In the baseline, the front wing contributes too much of the total downforce, while the floor contributes far less than it would on a modern F1 car. That balance is itself evidence that the pilot mesh and high-$y^+$ treatment do not resolve the underfloor flow adequately.

The realizable $k$-$\epsilon$ and SST cases differ by only 2.8% in full-car $C_l$, but their local pressure differences are much larger than those in the strongest roughness case. Large component-level changes can cancel when added into a full-car total.

![Component-load changes across the variants](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/component_sensitivity_heatmap.png)

Vortex visualisations also depend on an arbitrary threshold. Sweeping the $Q$ threshold from $10^4$ to $2\times10^6$ reduced the number of isosurface points from about 125,000 to 3,900. Any image made with one threshold is only a single slice through a continuous range of choices.

## The lap-time model uses relative changes only

The pilot model produces unrealistically low absolute downforce. I therefore do not feed its absolute coefficients directly into the lap-time model.

Instead, I apply each setup’s change relative to baseline to representative values of CLA 4.5 and CDA 1.6.

![Aerodynamic trade-offs on two circuit types](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/lap_trade.png)

A circuit with a high cornering share favours the maximum-downforce setting. On a circuit with a high straight-line share, the optimum lies between the extremes rather than at the minimum-drag endpoint.

I use public qualifying telemetry only to check the general structure of the trade-off between straight-line speed and low-speed cornering speed. It does not provide absolute validation against any real team.

## What this study still cannot answer

The difference between this third-party geometry and the real car is unknown. I have no wind-tunnel or track correlation, and the mesh study does not show that downforce has entered an asymptotic convergence range.

High $y^+$, closed cooling paths, first-order schemes, and the pilot-scale mesh all affect component loads. The ride-height cliff and front-wing load loss still require finer meshes, longer URANS or DES runs, and coupled body motion.

These results can identify mechanisms worth further study and explanations that the evidence has already rejected. They cannot establish how much downforce a real RB22 produces.

## The method I kept

The most important outcomes were not individual coefficients, but three withdrawals and corrections:

- Stable solver settings overturned my claim that the second-order scheme itself was unstable.
- URANS overturned my interpretation that steady divergence at +20 mm demonstrated real unsteadiness.
- I withdrew the entire first yaw sweep after finding incorrect tyre rotation centres.

All three errors produced outputs that initially looked plausible. Only comparison cases, numerical-uncertainty estimates, and symmetry checks exposed them.

What remains is an aerodynamic map with explicit boundaries—not a set of pilot-scale numbers presented as real-car performance.
