## How it started

It began when I got hold of a CAD model of the F1 2026 RB 22 online and tried to do CFD research on it with OpenFOAM. This was my first time combining OpenFOAM with an F1 2026 car. The CAD model was rebuilt from a third-party model, not the real car, so the specific numbers carry no particular reference value. This is mainly a study log of mine.

My idea was to vary parameters like the numerical scheme, front and rear wing angles, tyre contact patch area, ride height, rake, and yaw angle, and study their effects on the F1 car's drag, downforce, and front-to-rear balance. In the end I successfully ran 37 cases. Another 4 yaw cases failed at the start because of a wrong tyre boundary condition.

My main baseline mesh has about 4.35 million cells, and most steady cases use a first-order scheme with high $y^+$ wall functions. I also found no wind-tunnel or track-correlation data, which is one reason my conclusions cannot back up real-world performance.

## Model and computational workflow

The original CAD geometry consists of 14 sub-STEP components. The full car is 5.40 m long, 1.88 m wide, with a 3.40 m wheelbase. I used a 50 m/s freestream and controlled the moving ground. The workflow:

```text
STEP geometry
→ sewing and triangulation
→ snappyHexMesh
→ moving ground and rotating tyres
→ OpenFOAM 14 steady RANS or URANS
→ mesh, conservation, log, and force-window checks
→ full-car and component results
```

## Studying numerical uncertainty

I used three meshes:

| Mesh | Cells | $C_d$ | $C_l$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Coarse | 592,322 | 0.319 | −0.134 |
| Medium | 2,032,685 | 0.260 | −0.252 |
| Fine | 4,351,624 | 0.241 | −0.250 |

I found that drag changes monotonically as the mesh gets finer, but the downforce on the medium mesh overshoots the fine-mesh result. A 4th mesh level exceeded my computer's 15 GB memory limit, so with the data I have now, I cannot prove mesh independence.

As for the tails of the force curves, they look smooth, with short-window confidence intervals of about 0.3%–0.6%. But if we extend the averaging window from the last 50 iterations to the last 400, the mean changes by 15%–19%. With block resampling, the downforce uncertainty is about 3%–5%. So from then on, I only treated a physical change as a clear trend when it clearly exceeded that scale.

## The effect of numerical schemes

When I first ran the second-order Linear Upwind scheme, the case immediately threw a floating-point exception, and at the time I thought this mesh simply could not use a second-order scheme. Later I switched to a more stable solver setup, and the same 4.35-million-cell mesh finished 800 iterations without trouble. The results:

| Scheme             | $C_d$ | $C_l$  |
|----------------|------:|-------:|
| First-order upwind    | 0.241 | −0.250 |
| `linearUpwind` | 0.237 | −0.236 |

The second-order scheme had 1.8% less drag and 5.6% less downforce. This shows the earlier failure was a solver-setup problem, not that the second-order scheme itself was unusable. So first-order results seem fine for relative comparisons within the same configuration, but every conclusion has to carry this roughly 6% numerical-scheme difference with it.

## The rear wing study

I swept the rear wing angle from $+8^\circ$ all the way to $-12^\circ$, to see whether reducing drag and adding downforce could be adjusted separately.

![Six-point rear-wing polar](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/wing_polar.png)

The final data:

- When I rotated to $-12^\circ$, rear-wing downforce was 38% above baseline, but drag was also 74% higher, and it still had not stalled.
- When I rotated toward drag reduction, past $+4^\circ$, each extra degree removed very little additional drag. I found the reason: the floor lifts the airflow upward, so the angle the rear wing actually feels is smaller than what was rotated away. In Straight Mode, the drag it can save has an upper limit.

Across the whole sweep, drag varied by at most 11% and downforce by at most 23%.

My conclusion is that low drag and high downforce are not two independent switches. The rear wing has to take care of drag, downforce, and front-to-rear balance at the same time.

## The front wing study

| Setting | $C_d$ | $C_l$ | Front-axle share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Front wing unloaded $+4^\circ$ | 0.2408 | −0.2079 | 8.6% |
| Baseline | 0.2413 | −0.2498 | 24.9% |
| Front wing incidence increased $-4^\circ$ | 0.2437 | −0.2328 | 22.2% |

Here the front wing acts more like a balance knob that can only be turned one way. After unloading by $+4^\circ$, the front-axle share fell from the baseline 24.9% to 8.6%, a loss of 16.3 percentage points. The effect is very obvious.

Going the other way and adding incidence did not work: the front-wing leading edge was left with only 34 mm of ground clearance, the front wing's own downforce actually fell by about 10%, and its drag rose by about 30%. In this direction the geometry has already hit its ground-effect limit, and there is nothing more to add.

I also worked out that rotating the front wing about $+2^\circ$ would recover the rearward balance shift caused by the rear wing at $+8^\circ$. In the combined case using both, the front-axle share came back to 23.9%, only 1 percentage point from baseline, but the cost was 22.7% less total downforce.

![Front and rear wing settings against full-car balance](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/balance_map.png)

## The tyre contact patch study

In the original model, each tyre touched the ground along a single line, as thin as a knife edge. That is not how it really is: because of gravity, a real tyre has a contact patch. After I added a 15 mm tall plinth under the tyres, the high-pressure region piled up in front of the front wheels and the airflow squeezed out to the sides both became smaller.

The result: full-car drag fell by 6.1%, downforce rose by 4.4%, and $-L/D$ improved from 1.035 to 1.152, an 11% gain. This was the biggest payoff of all the single modifications.

![Original contact-region pressure](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/baseline_z035_cp.png)

![Contact-region pressure with a 15 mm plinth](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/plinth15_z035_cp.png)

The 5 mm and 10 mm plinths also reduced drag, but their downforce changes still fell inside the 3%–5% uncertainty, so this part is not clear enough yet. Only at 15 mm were the drag and downforce changes more or less clear enough.

I also tried pressing the tyres down by 2, 4, 6, and 8 mm. It turned out harder pressing did not always mean less downforce: at 6 mm of compression the downforce loss was 6.1%, while at 8 mm the loss was only 2.9%. So for now I cannot summarize a simple rule here.

## The ride height study

| Body lift | $C_d$ | $C_l$ | Floor $C_l$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 mm | 0.2413 | −0.2498 | −0.0644 |
| +10 mm | 0.2309 | −0.2222 | −0.0571 |
| +15 mm | 0.2079 | −0.1636 | −0.0208 |
| +20 mm | Steady solver diverged twice | — | — |

Raising the body the first 10 mm cut total downforce by 11%. Raising it another 5 mm lost a further 26%, and the downforce produced by the floor dropped by a full 64%. The range between 10 and 15 mm is like a cliff; above it the floor basically stops working.

![Floor load loss across the ride-height sweep](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/ride_map.png)

At +20 mm, the steady solver diverged twice. At the time I guessed the flow field itself had become unsteady. Later I re-ran it with URANS and found that both +15 mm and +20 mm advanced smoothly, with force oscillations even smaller than baseline. So my guess now is that the earlier divergence was the SIMPLE solver failing on its own, not real porpoising, and for now it cannot be taken as evidence of unsteadiness.

## The yaw study

For the first version of the yaw cases, I treated the left and right tyres as the same rotating surface, sharing one centre of rotation. The result: downforce at $+3^\circ$ to the left and $-3^\circ$ to the right differed by 13.5%. Left and right were asymmetric, which was clearly wrong, so at that point I withdrew all four cases.

In the second version I gave each of the four tyres its own rotating surface and centre of rotation. The recomputed data:

| Yaw angle | $C_d$ | $C_l$ | Front-axle share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| $-3^\circ$ | 0.2768 | −0.1985 | 37.2% |
| $0^\circ$ | 0.2769 | −0.2089 | 25.7% |
| $+3^\circ$ | 0.2754 | −0.1867 | 38.0% |

Finally I averaged the left and right directions: at yaw $|3^\circ|$, drag was basically unchanged (about −0.3%), downforce fell by 7.8%, and the front-axle share rose by 11.9 percentage points.

![Corrected full-car yaw results](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/yawv2_map.png)

## The rake study

I pitched the body nose-down and nose-up by $0.3^\circ$ each, around the front-tyre contact point, keeping the tyres on the ground:

- Nose-down $-0.3^\circ$: downforce fell by 11.4%;
- Nose-up $+0.3^\circ$: downforce fell by 19.7%, and the front-axle share rose by 9.2 percentage points.

![Rake sweep](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/rake_map.png)

Both directions lost downforce, so this model cannot be summarized as "more rake means more floor downforce". I think here, front-wing ground clearance, tyre squeeze flow, and the floor inlet together decide a narrow and asymmetric attitude window.

## Full-car totals alone are not the whole picture

In the baseline, the front wing's share of the downforce is on the high side, and the floor's share is clearly lower than on a modern F1 car. That in itself shows my pilot mesh and high $y^+$ wall functions did not compute the underfloor flow accurately.

Another example: the realizable $k$-$\epsilon$ and SST turbulence models differ by only 2.8% in full-car $C_l$, but their local pressure differences are larger than in the strongest tyre-roughness case. Large changes in the components cancel each other out in the total force; you cannot see them by looking only at the total.

![Component load changes across the variants](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/component_sensitivity_heatmap.png)

Vortex-structure plots are the same story. Sweeping the $Q$ threshold from $10^4$ to $2\times10^6$ shrinks the visible isosurface from about 125,000 points to 3,900. So any single vortex plot is just one slice at a man-made threshold, not the one and only truth.

![Q-criterion three-dimensional vortex-structure isosurface](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/qcrit_threequarter.jpg)

## The lap-time study

The absolute downforce from my pilot model is on the low side, so the lap-time model cannot use these absolute coefficients directly. I could only apply each setup's change relative to baseline onto representative values of CLA 4.5 and CDA 1.6.

![Aerodynamic trade-offs on two types of circuit](/images/projects/f1-2026-aero/campaign/lap_trade.png)

The results show: a circuit with many corners favours the maximum-downforce setting, while on a circuit with many straights the optimum sits in the middle, not at the minimum-drag end.

## The flaws of this round

Counting carefully, there are quite a lot of flaws.

How far this third-party CAD geometry really is from the real car is still unknown. There was no (and no chance of getting) wind-tunnel or track data to compare against. The mesh study also did not prove the downforce had converged.

High $y^+$, closed cooling paths, first-order schemes, and a pilot-scale mesh: all of these change component loads. The two phenomena of the ride-height cliff and the front-wing load loss still need finer meshes, longer URANS or DES, and coupled body motion to be figured out. For that I need to verify them on a higher-performance computer.

So the conclusions I have now can tell me which mechanisms are worth studying further and which things have already been ruled out. They cannot answer how much downforce a real car produces at each location, but they point the direction for my further research.

## What this round achieved

The most important gain this time was not any single number, but the research process, because I overturned myself many times along the way:

- I thought the second-order scheme was unstable, but switching to a stable solver setup fixed it;
- I guessed the +20 mm divergence was real unsteadiness, but URANS proved the solver just could not handle it;
- I thought the first yaw version was usable, but the tyre rotation centres were wrong, so I had to withdraw all of it.

All three mistakes produced plausible-looking results at the time. They were exposed only through comparison cases, uncertainty estimates, and left-right symmetry checks.

I think there is a great, great deal worth studying. An F1 car is like a vast ocean, and I am very eager to write a second study about racing cars.
